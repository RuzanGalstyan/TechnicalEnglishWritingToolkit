The paragraph under the figure. Twenty exercises on trend language, prepositions and how much to interpret.

Why this matters

Your report has a figure in it. Now you must write the paragraph underneath — the one that tells the reader what the figure means, without reading the numbers aloud.

Redundant"In 2019 it was 42. In 2020 it was 47. In 2021 it was 51."The reader can see all of this. If your text only repeats the figure, one of the two is unnecessary.
Doing work"Consumption rose steadily to a peak of 71 in August, an increase of roughly 69%, before levelling off at around 55."Shape, two numbers as evidence, and the pattern named.

The three-move paragraph

  1. Point to the figure and state the headline. Figure 3 shows a steady increase over the five-year period.
  2. Give supporting detail. The largest change, the turning point, the outlier — with numbers.
  3. Interpret. What caused it, what it implies, or what limitation applies.
Rule of thumb: describe the shape, quote two or three numbers as evidence, and spend the rest on what it means. If a marker could delete your paragraph without losing information, it was not doing any work.

Two ways to say the same thing

Verb + adverbAdjective + noun
Output rose sharply.There was a sharp rise in output.
Emissions declined slightly.There was a slight decline in emissions.

Alternate between them. Using only one pattern makes a data paragraph repetitive within three sentences.

Calibrate your adverbs

SizeWords
Very smallmarginally · slightly
Steadysteadily · gradually · consistently
Largesharply · markedly · substantially
Very largedramatically · considerably

Dramatically is overused. Save it for changes that genuinely are dramatic, or it loses all force.

Two traps in one line. Aumentó en un 12% gives increased in a 12% — English uses by with no article. And gráfico is a graph or chart; an English “graphic” is a picture.
