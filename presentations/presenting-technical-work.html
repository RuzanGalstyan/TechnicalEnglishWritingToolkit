Giving a Technical
Presentation

Signposting, handling questions, and how to say numbers, units and equations out loud. Twenty exercises.

Designed by Ruzana Galstyan-Sargsyan · Universitat Politècnica de València

Why this matters

A reader who loses the thread scrolls back. A listener who loses it is lost for the rest of your talk. Everything that makes technical speaking different follows from that one fact — including the part nobody teaches: how to say your own numbers out loud.

Never said aloud before"two comma five times ten minus three meter"You have read 2.5 × 10⁻³ m for years without ever pronouncing it. A defence is a poor place to find out.
Said correctly"two point five times ten to the minus three metres"Decimal point, standard power form, plural unit.

Signposting

These phrases tell the listener where they are. Learn twenty and you will use them for the rest of your career.

MomentSay
AimThe aim of this project was to determine whether…
StructureI've divided the talk into three parts. First… then… and finally…
TransitionThat covers the background. Let's turn to the method.
A figureThis graph shows… · The key point here is…
EmphasisThe most important result is that…
ClosingThat concludes my presentation. I'd be happy to answer any questions.
Decode the figure before you interpret it. Say what the axes are and what the units are, then point at the pattern. A listener still decoding the axes cannot follow your explanation.

Decimal points are a real hazard. Much of continental Europe writes 3,5 where English writes 3.5, and English uses the comma for thousands. Say “three comma five” to an international audience and some will hear thirty-five.

Questions

SituationSay
You didn't hearI'm sorry, could you repeat the question?
Checking you understoodIf I understand correctly, you're asking whether…?
You need a momentThat's a good question. Let me think for a second.
You don't knowI don't have data on that, but I would expect…

Saying you don't know is not a failure. Inventing an answer in front of an examiner who knows the field is much worse, and they can always tell.

Fluency is not the criterion. Technical audiences are used to accented and non-native English — most of the world's engineering is conducted in it. Being clear, structured and audible matters far more, and slowing down is the single most effective improvement available to almost every student presenter.
