Technical writing

Proposals

The only technical document whose job is to be accepted. Twenty exercises on scope, method, resources and the language of a plan.

Designed by Ruzana Galstyan-Sargsyan · Universitat Politècnica de València

Why this matters

A proposal asks someone to give you something — funding, equipment, access, or permission to do your project. It is the only technical document whose job is to be accepted, and it is judged on whether the reader believes you can deliver.

Gets rejected"We would like to study ventilation systems because it is an interesting and important topic."No problem, no method, no scope, nothing the reader can say yes to.
Gets approved"Three UPV teaching buildings run fixed-schedule ventilation. We propose to instrument two rooms and compare occupancy-based control over one semester, using equipment already held by the department."Problem, scope, method, and a resource question already answered.

What a proposal must do

Every proposal answers four questions, whether or not it has headings for them.

QuestionWhat convinces the reader
What is the problem?Something specific and real, not a topic area
What will you do about it?A method concrete enough to picture
Can you actually do it?Time, equipment, access, skills — named, not assumed
Why is it worth doing?What changes if it works

Four kinds

  • Solicited — someone asked for it, usually against a brief. Follow their structure exactly.
  • Unsolicited — your idea, nobody asked. You must first convince the reader a problem exists.
  • Internal — to your own department or company. Shorter; shared context assumed.
  • External — to a client or funder. Longer; nothing assumed.
A TFG proposal is solicited and internal. Your school publishes a required structure. Follow it exactly, even where this page suggests otherwise — a proposal that ignores the brief is rejected before anyone reads the idea.

Typical sections

SectionJob
TitleNames the specific problem, not the field
BackgroundEstablishes that the problem is real, with evidence
ObjectivesWhat you will produce, in numbered points
ScopeWhat you will not do. The section most often missing
MethodHow, concretely, with what
ScheduleDates or a Gantt chart, with milestones
ResourcesEquipment, access, budget, supervision
Expected outcomesWhat the reader gets

Why proposals get rejected

  • Scope too broad. The commonest reason. A reader who cannot see the edges cannot believe the timeline.
  • Vague method. "We will analyse the data" tells nobody whether you know how.
  • Resources assumed. If you need a machine, say whose and whether it is free.
  • No stated problem. An interesting topic is not a problem.
  • Ignoring the brief. Missing sections read as carelessness.

Language: future, but committed

WeakBetter
We would like to try to measure…We will measure…
It could perhaps be possible to test…We will test…
Hopefully the results will show…The results will indicate whether…
Hedge your findings, not your plan. You do not yet know what you will find, so hedge that. But you do know what you intend to do — so say it in plain future tense. A hedged plan reads as an unfinished one.

For Spanish speakers

From SpanishEnglish
We pretend to measure the airflow.We intend to measure the airflow.
The actual system uses fixed schedules.The current system uses fixed schedules.
We dispose of two flow meters.We have two flow meters available.
This project will permit to reduce consumption.This project will make it possible to reduce consumption.
We will realise a survey of the users.We will carry out a survey of the users.
Pretender is not pretend. Pretendemos medir means we intend to measure. English pretend means to act falsely — in a proposal it is an unfortunate error to make.
