Problem-Solving Emails

Something has gone wrong and someone has to act. Twenty-five exercises on reporting a fault, saying what you did, and asking for what you need.

Designed by Ruzana Galstyan-Sargsyan · Universitat Politècnica de València

Why this matters

On placement, in your project, at work: a machine stops, a part arrives wrong, data is missing. You have to email someone who can fix it — and that person is busy.

Gets ignored"Sorry to bother you. The conveyor is not working very well. Could someone look at it sometime?"No symptom, no evidence, no deadline. The reader cannot act, so they postpone it.
Gets a technician"The line 3 conveyor stopped twice yesterday, both within ten minutes of a changeover. I reset the drive and checked belt tension — within spec. Could you send a technician before Friday's run?"Symptom, evidence, what you tried, what you need, by when.

EvidenceNumbers that make it credibleThe reading reached… · Both stops occurred…
Action takenStops repeated suggestionsI have already… · I checked X, which was…
RequestWho does what, by whenCould you…? · Would it be possible to…?

Three habits that lose you the reader

Apologising instead of reporting. "Sorry to bother you, sorry for the inconvenience…" Say the problem. One brief apology at the end if genuinely needed.
Burying the request. If the thing you need is in the middle of a long paragraph, it will be missed. Give it its own sentence, near the end.
Deadlines with no reason. "I need it today" reads as a demand. "Could you reply by 16:00? The report is due tomorrow" reads as information.

Softening a request

RegisterPhrasing
NeutralCould you send the datasheet?
PoliteCould you possibly send the datasheet?
FormalI would be grateful if you could send the datasheet.

The bigger the favour, the longer the grammar around it.

Watch duda. In English, doubt means you suspect something is untrue. When you mean una duda, say question.
