Why this matters

Every technical degree asks you to explain how something works — a treatment plant, a production line, an algorithm, a test procedure. The grammar is the same in all of them, and it is not the grammar you use for instructions.

Wrong genre"First you connect the sensor, then you start the software."That is an instruction. It tells one person what to do.
Process description"The sensor is connected and the software is then started."The equipment stays in focus, and the description holds true whoever runs it.

Why the machine gets the spotlight

In everyday English the person acts first: the operator opens the valve. In technical description that flips, because the reader is following the material through the system, not the staff: the valve is opened.

The test: if the answer to “who does this?” is “whoever is on duty”, use the passive. If it genuinely matters who acts — the supervisor signs off the report — use the active.

Sequencers — vary them

Initially · First · In the first stage · Next · Then · After this · Subsequently · At this point · Once this is complete · Finally

A paragraph of First… Then… Then… Then… reads like a recipe written for a child.

Two tenses, two jobs

UseTenseExample
How something normally workspresent simple passiveThe sample is heated to 180 °C.
What you did in your own projectpast simple passiveThe sample was heated to 180 °C.

Manuals and general descriptions use the present; method sections use the past. Mixing them is the commonest fault in student reports.

The se problem again. Spanish impersonal se maps onto the English passive with the thing affected as subject — not onto it plus an active verb.
