Why this matters

Assume your reader has forty unread messages and eleven seconds for yours. That single assumption produces most of the rules — and English politeness is built from grammar, so a grammatically perfect email can still land as rude and simply get no reply.

No reply"Send me the datasheet for part 3302."Grammatically correct. To a stranger it reads as an order, and orders from strangers get ignored.
Gets the datasheet"Could you send me the datasheet for part 3302?"Same request. One modal makes it askable.

Six parts

The politeness ladder

RegisterPhrasing
DirectPlease send the datasheet.
NeutralCould you send me the datasheet?
PoliteCould you possibly send me the datasheet?
FormalI would be grateful if you could send me the datasheet.
The calibration: the bigger the favour, the longer the grammar around the request. If timing matters, give a deadline and a reason — a deadline alone reads as a demand.

Greetings

SituationUse
You know the nameDear Ms Serrano, / Dear Marta,
You do notDear Sir or Madam, / Dear Recruitment Team,
Established contactHi Marta,

Ms is the safe default. Dear Mr Marta and Dear Mrs Serrano (when you do not know her marital status) are both common errors. Mirror the register your reader uses.

Sign-offs

Kind regards (safe everywhere) · Best regards · Yours sincerely (named reader, formal) · Yours faithfully (Dear Sir or Madam)

Writing to other non-native speakers? Much of your professional email will be. Plain, unambiguous English beats idiomatic English — use postpone rather than put off, and never use humour or sarcasm to soften a difficult message.

For Spanish speakers

From SpanishEnglish
I am agree with your proposal.I agree with your proposal.
I will assist to the meeting.I will attend the meeting.
Attached you can find my CV.I have attached my CV.
I look forward to hear from you.I look forward to hearing from you.
Sorry for the inconveniences.Sorry for the inconvenience.
I remain at your disposal for any doubt.Please let me know if you have any questions.
Duda is a question. English doubt means you suspect something is untrue. “If you have any doubts” tells your reader you expect them to distrust you.
