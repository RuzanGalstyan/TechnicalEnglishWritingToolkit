Why this matters

A vague prompt gets a vague answer, and a vague answer wastes more time than writing the thing yourself. The difference is almost always context and specificity.

Wastes your time"Can you help me with my report?"You get generic advice about report writing. Nothing you can act on.
Useful in one go"Check whether the aim in this paragraph answers the gap in the previous one. Do not rewrite."One task, your own text, and a diagnosis you can act on.

Part 1

Who should it act as?

Setting a role narrows the vocabulary and assumptions. Useful for technical topics, unnecessary for simple tasks.

Part 2

What is the task?

The one thing that must be right. Use a specific verb — explain, compare, check, outline — not "help me with".

Part 3

What context does it need?

Your level, your field, what you already know, what you have tried. This is the part that most improves the answer, and the part most people skip.

Part 4

Any constraints?

Length, register, what to avoid. Select any that apply.

Explain statistics.Explain when to use a paired t-test rather than an unpaired one, with an engineering example.

Context is the highest-value part

"Explain regression" and "Explain regression — I'm a second-year student who understands correlation but not why the residuals matter" produce very different answers. The second tells the tool where to start, so it does not spend three paragraphs on things you already know.

Ask for reasoning, not just conclusions

Adding explain your reasoning or show the steps produces an answer you can actually check. A bare conclusion is impossible to verify and teaches you nothing.

Iterate rather than restart

If the answer is close but wrong in one respect, say which respect. "That's too general — give me a concrete example from manufacturing" is faster and better than rewriting your prompt from scratch.

The most useful constraint for students. "Do not write any text for me to submit — explain it so that I can write it myself." It keeps the tool on the side of learning, and it keeps you out of the situation where you own words you cannot defend.

Prompts worth keeping

Read this paragraph and tell me, for each sentence, whether it reports a result or interprets one. Do not rewrite it. I think my gap statement and my aim don't match. Explain the mismatch — don't fix it. Ask me five questions a marker might ask about this method section.

All three ask for diagnosis rather than production. Diagnostic prompts are almost always permitted, and they are the ones that make you better.

Before you use anything it gives you

Check your assignment brief first. Rules differ between institutions, programmes and individual assignments, and they are changing quickly. This tool builds prompts; it does not tell you whether you are permitted to use one. See When Is AI Use Acceptable?.

Three failure modes to expect

  • Invented references. Plausible authors, real-sounding journals, correct-looking years — for papers that do not exist. Verify every citation against the actual source before it goes anywhere near your reference list.
  • Confident errors. Fluency and accuracy are unrelated. A wrong explanation reads exactly like a right one, which is why asking for reasoning matters: it gives you something to check.
  • Averaged, generic prose. Output tends towards the middle of everything it has seen. That is the opposite of what a good technical report needs, which is your specifics.

Never paste in

  • Unpublished company or placement data, unless you have explicit permission
  • Personal data about identifiable people
  • Anything covered by an NDA or a confidentiality agreement
  • Other people's unpublished work — a classmate's draft, a supervisor's manuscript
The test that outlasts the technology. Could you explain every sentence you submit to someone who asked? That question was the right one before these tools existed and it will still be right when they have changed again.
